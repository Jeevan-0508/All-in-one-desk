# Chapter 07: Risk Taxonomy

---

## 7.1 What Is Risk Taxonomy

Risk taxonomy is a **structured classification system** used to:
- Categorize risks consistently
- Ensure full coverage
- Avoid overlap and ambiguity
- Enable reporting and analysis

A taxonomy answers:
> “What kinds of risks exist in this organization?”

Without taxonomy:
- Risks are mislabeled
- Gaps go unnoticed
- Analysis becomes inconsistent

---

## 7.2 Why Risk Taxonomy Is Critical

Risk taxonomy enables:
- Common language
- Comparable assessments
- Aggregated reporting
- Targeted controls

If one team calls something “operational” and another calls it “cyber,”
governance breaks down.

---

## 7.3 Hierarchical Structure of Risk Taxonomy

Risk taxonomy is usually **multi-level**:

- Level 1: Risk Category
- Level 2: Risk Subcategory
- Level 3: Risk Drivers / Themes

Example:
- Cyber Risk
  - Access Control Risk
    - Excessive Privileges

---

## 7.4 Level 1: Enterprise Risk Categories

Typical Level 1 categories include:

1. Strategic Risk
2. Operational Risk
3. Financial Risk
4. Compliance & Legal Risk
5. Cyber / Information Security Risk
6. Reputational Risk
7. Supply Chain Risk
8. Third-Party Risk
9. Health, Safety & Environment Risk

These categories are **organization-wide**.

---

## 7.5 Strategic Risk

Strategic risks affect:
- Long-term objectives
- Competitive position
- Business model viability

Examples:
- Poor strategic decisions
- Market disruption
- M&A failures
- Regulatory shifts

Strategic risks are owned by leadership.

---

## 7.6 Operational Risk

Operational risks arise from:
- Processes
- People
- Systems
- Internal failures

Examples:
- Process breakdowns
- Manual errors
- Capacity constraints
- SOP non-compliance

Operational risk is the **most common category**.

---

## 7.7 Financial Risk

Financial risks relate to:
- Revenue
- Costs
- Cash flow
- Assets

Examples:
- Fraud
- Pricing errors
- Credit risk
- Budget overruns

Financial risk often overlaps with operational risk.

---

## 7.8 Compliance and Legal Risk

Compliance risks arise from:
- Regulatory requirements
- Legal obligations
- Internal policies

Examples:
- Regulatory penalties
- Data protection violations
- Contract breaches

Compliance risk is often **lagging**—it materializes after failure.

---

## 7.9 Cyber / Information Security Risk

Cyber risk relates to:
- Confidentiality
- Integrity
- Availability of information

Examples:
- Unauthorized access
- Data breaches
- Ransomware
- System outages

Cyber risk is **not purely technical**.
It includes people and process failures.

👉 This category will be expanded in **Chapter 26**.

---

## 7.10 Reputational Risk

Reputational risk arises when:
- Stakeholder trust is damaged
- Public perception deteriorates

Examples:
- Public incidents
- Ethical failures
- Poor customer experience

Reputational risk is often a **consequence**, not a root cause.

---

## 7.11 Supply Chain Risk

Supply chain risks involve:
- Vendors
- Logistics
- Dependencies
- External partners

Examples:
- Vendor failure
- Delays
- Quality issues
- Geopolitical disruptions

Supply chain risk became critical post-2020.

---

## 7.12 Third-Party Risk

Third-party risk arises when:
- External entities act on behalf of the organization
- Control is indirect

Examples:
- Vendor data breaches
- Outsourced process failures
- Contractual non-compliance

Third-party risk extends internal risk externally.

---

## 7.13 Risk Interdependencies

Risks rarely exist in isolation.

Example:
- Cyber risk → Operational disruption → Reputational damage → Financial loss

Taxonomy helps track **primary vs secondary risks**.

---

## 7.14 Customizing the Taxonomy

Taxonomies must be adapted to:
- Industry
- Regulatory environment
- Organizational complexity

Overly generic taxonomies hide risk.
Overly detailed taxonomies create noise.

Balance is critical.

---

## 7.15 Summary

Risk taxonomy:
- Creates a common risk language
- Enables structured analysis
- Supports governance and reporting
- Prevents blind spots

Every risk must map to a taxonomy category.
