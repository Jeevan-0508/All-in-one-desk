# Chapter 25: Incident Management and Escalation

---

## 25.1 What Is an Incident

An incident is an event where:
- A risk materializes, or
- Controls fail, or
- Objectives are disrupted

Not all incidents cause damage, but all indicate control stress.

---

## 25.2 Risk vs Incident

| Risk | Incident |
|----|---------|
| Potential | Actual |
| Forward-looking | Event-based |
| Preventable | Recoverable |

Risk management aims to prevent incidents.  
Incident management aims to respond effectively.

---

## 25.3 Purpose of Incident Management

Incident management exists to:
- Minimize impact
- Restore operations
- Protect stakeholders
- Preserve evidence
- Enable learning

Speed matters, but structure matters more.

---

## 25.4 Incident Lifecycle

A standard incident lifecycle includes:

1. Detection
2. Reporting
3. Classification
4. Containment
5. Resolution
6. Recovery
7. Review

Skipping steps creates blind spots.

---

## 25.5 Incident Detection

Incidents may be detected through:
- Monitoring systems
- Employee reporting
- Customer complaints
- Audit findings
- External notifications

Early detection reduces impact.

---

## 25.6 Incident Classification

Incidents should be classified by:
- Severity
- Impact type
- Affected area
- Regulatory relevance

Classification drives escalation.

---

## 25.7 Escalation Principles

Escalation ensures:
- The right people are informed
- Decisions are made at the right level
- Risks are contained quickly

Escalation is a control, not a failure.

---

## 25.8 Escalation Triggers

Common escalation triggers include:
- Breach of risk tolerance
- Regulatory exposure
- Customer harm
- Financial loss thresholds
- Reputational risk

Triggers must be predefined.

---

## 25.9 Escalation Paths

Escalation paths define:
- Who is notified
- In what timeframe
- Through which channels

Unclear paths delay response.

---

## 25.10 Roles and Responsibilities

Incident response roles may include:
- Incident owner
- Technical responders
- Business owners
- Legal and compliance
- Communications teams

Role clarity prevents confusion.

---

## 25.11 Communication During Incidents

Effective communication must be:
- Timely
- Accurate
- Controlled
- Audience-specific

Poor communication amplifies harm.

---

## 25.12 Incident Documentation

Documentation should capture:
- Timeline of events
- Decisions made
- Actions taken
- Evidence preserved

Documentation supports learning and accountability.

---

## 25.13 Post-Incident Review

After resolution, organizations must:
- Identify root causes
- Assess control effectiveness
- Define corrective actions
- Update the risk register

Incidents are learning opportunities.

---

## 25.14 Incident Trends and Analysis

Analyzing incidents over time reveals:
- Weak controls
- Emerging risks
- Cultural issues

Trends matter more than isolated events.

---

## 25.15 Common Failures in Incident Management

Common failures include:
- Delayed escalation
- Informal handling
- Poor documentation
- Blame-focused reviews

Poor response compounds damage.

---

## 25.16 Incident Management and Governance

Governance ensures:
- Oversight of major incidents
- Accountability for failures
- Tracking of corrective actions

Incidents must reach leadership when appropriate.

---

## 25.17 Summary

Incident management:
- Limits damage
- Restores stability
- Enables learning
- Strengthens resilience

How you respond matters as much as what happened.
