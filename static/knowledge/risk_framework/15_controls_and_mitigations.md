# Chapter 15: Controls and Mitigations

---

## 15.1 What Are Controls

Controls are **measures implemented to manage risk** by:
- Preventing risk events
- Detecting risk events
- Reducing impact when events occur

Controls operationalize risk treatment decisions.

A risk without controls is unmanaged exposure.

---

## 15.2 Controls vs Mitigations

| Aspect | Controls | Mitigations |
|-----|---------|------------|
| Timing | Before or during events | After or during events |
| Purpose | Reduce likelihood | Reduce impact |
| Nature | Often preventive or detective | Often corrective |

In practice, the terms overlap, but intent matters.

---

## 15.3 Control Objectives

Every control must have a **clear objective**, such as:
- Prevent unauthorized access
- Detect anomalies
- Ensure compliance
- Maintain availability

Controls without objectives become meaningless activities.

---

## 15.4 Types of Controls by Function

### Preventive Controls
- Stop risk events from occurring
- Examples: Access restrictions, approvals, segregation of duties

### Detective Controls
- Identify when something goes wrong
- Examples: Monitoring, alerts, audits

### Corrective Controls
- Restore systems or processes
- Examples: Incident response, recovery procedures

Strong risk management uses all three.

---

## 15.5 Types of Controls by Nature

### Administrative Controls
- Policies
- Procedures
- Training
- Governance

### Technical Controls
- System configurations
- Automation
- Security tools

### Physical Controls
- Locks
- Access badges
- Surveillance

Over-reliance on one type weakens control posture.

---

## 15.6 Manual vs Automated Controls

| Manual Controls | Automated Controls |
|---------------|-------------------|
| Human-dependent | System-enforced |
| Flexible | Consistent |
| Error-prone | Scalable |

Automation improves reliability but still requires governance.

---

## 15.7 Control Design Principles

Effective controls should be:
- Proportionate to risk
- Clearly defined
- Easy to execute
- Hard to bypass
- Auditable

Poorly designed controls create workarounds.

---

## 15.8 Control Effectiveness

Control effectiveness depends on:
- Design adequacy
- Consistent execution
- Monitoring
- Owner accountability

A well-designed control that is not executed is ineffective.

---

## 15.9 Control Ownership

Every control must have:
- A named owner
- Clear responsibility
- Authority to enforce

Unowned controls degrade quickly.

---

## 15.10 Control Dependencies and Failure Modes

Controls often depend on:
- Other controls
- People
- Systems

Single-point-of-failure controls increase risk.

Redundancy improves resilience.

---

## 15.11 Control Testing and Assurance

Controls must be tested to ensure:
- They exist
- They work as intended
- They are followed

Testing methods include:
- Self-assessments
- Audits
- Continuous monitoring

Untested controls are assumed controls.

---

## 15.12 Common Control Failures

Typical control failures include:
- Outdated procedures
- Alert fatigue
- Excessive manual steps
- Misaligned incentives
- Lack of training

Controls fail when people stop believing in them.

---

## 15.13 Controls vs Risk Appetite

Controls should align with:
- Risk appetite
- Business objectives

Over-controlling low-risk areas wastes resources.
Under-controlling high-risk areas creates exposure.

---

## 15.14 Control Documentation

Control documentation should include:
- Purpose
- Owner
- Frequency
- Evidence
- Testing approach

Documentation supports governance and audits.

---

## 15.15 Summary

Controls and mitigations:
- Translate risk treatment into reality
- Require good design and ownership
- Must be monitored and tested
- Fail when misaligned with culture and incentives

Strong controls reduce incidents without blocking business.
